# Stop Chasing Bottoms; Choose the Trade-off

VolStrat's v5 rule should not try to re-enter early after sell-offs, because neither the outside evidence nor VolStrat's own pre-registered tests contain a signal that reliably tells the first leg of a real recovery from a bear-market rally. Three pre-registered re-entry designs on v5 (v8, v10, v11), plus an earlier broad re-entry rule on the Growth profile, all failed. None raised Sharpe at the pre-registered bar, and the best moved median three-month rebound capture by only a few points (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §15.5, §21–§23, data to 2026-11-03). The reason is structural. On US data for 1993–2026, the 11% of days that follow 21-day volatility above 25% contained **15 of the 20 worst days and 19 of the 20 best**, so the exposure cut that avoids the crash is the same cut that misses the rebound (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §22–§23, data to 2026-11-03). The literature agrees. The market's sharpest rebounds come in high-volatility "panic" states after declines ([Daniel & Moskowitz, NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439)). Most published bottom signals are in-sample or scored by vendors. The best independent test of a price rule found that follow-through days succeed only about 55% of the time ([Quantifiable Edges](https://quantifiableedges.com/follow-through-days-quantified/)). The cost is real: after eleven major lows, v5 earned a **median of about 26% of the S&P 500's three-month rebound, and only 3% after March 2009**. That lag is the premium paid for a −20.4% maximum drawdown against the S&P's −55.2%. The honest options are to set how much of that premium to pay with the target-volatility and trend-brake dials, to consider a tranched glide path or breadth data only if clean data can be found, and to let the live record judge. A better trigger is not one of the options.

## v5 holds 10–25% equity just when the rebound runs fastest

The rule is simple, and so is the arithmetic of its lag. Equity is min(100%, 12% ÷ 21-day realised volatility), halved while the S&P 500 is below its 200-day average. At 36% volatility, the average on high-volatility days, that gives 33% equity, or **17% once the trend brake halves it**. At 60% volatility it gives 10%, and at 25% it gives 24% (my arithmetic from the published rule). Two locks have to open before v5 is fully invested again. The volatility lock opens slowly because the 21-day window keeps the crash days in the estimate for a full month of trading. After both the 2009-03-09 and 2020-03-23 lows, 21-day volatility first fell back below 25% on 20 May, when the S&P 500 was already **up 33.8% and 33.2%** (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §22–§23, data to 2026-11-03). The trend lock opens only when the index regains its 200-day average, which after a deep fall takes months. A 10-point rebalancing band adds a little more delay.

The pattern in the table matters more than the median. **Capture is worst in the deepest, fastest recoveries** (2009 and 2020, the two biggest rebounds, gave 3% and 17%). It is best after shallow shocks such as August 2024, when volatility never rose high enough to cut exposure much. A static 60/40 portfolio captured a median of about 60% of the same rebounds, simply because it never left (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §21, data to 2026-11-03). The other side of the ledger is just as clear. v5 passed its pre-registered test on 1927–1989, data the project had never used, with Sharpe ratios of **0.81 vs 0.47 (1927–1958) and 0.46 vs 0.37 (1959–1989)** against a static mix at the same average exposure. On 1993–2026 it earned a Sharpe of 0.65 against 0.54 for 60/40, with a −20.4% maximum drawdown against −35.8% for 60/40 and −55.2% for the S&P 500. On the 1927–1989 data, the trend brake alone added 0.14 Sharpe and the volatility budget alone 0.12 (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §18, data to 2026-11-03). The brake is the part that pays in slow bear markets, and it is also one of the two locks that hold v5 out of V-shaped recoveries. The protection and the lag come from the same machinery.

## The best and worst days arrive together, and only hindsight sorts them

Four structural facts make early re-entry hard. They reinforce one another, which is why five separate fixes failed in similar ways.

### Volatility clusters both tails into the same weeks

On US data for 1993–2026, high-volatility days earned the S&P 500 **7.1% a year at 36.4% volatility, against 11.2% at 14.8% on calmer days**. Calm days paid about three times as much per unit of risk, so stepping aside in stress is right on average. Yet the same 11% of days held 19 of the 20 best days (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §22–§23, data to 2026-11-03). The academic record shows the same structure. Daniel and Moskowitz, using US data from 1927 to 2013, find that momentum crashes happen in panic states, after market declines and while volatility is high, at the same time as sharp market rebounds ([NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439)). In bear markets momentum behaves like a written call on the market: it gains a little as prices fall and loses a lot when they snap back ([Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/understanding-momentum-crashes)). Bollerslev, Tauchen and Zhou find that a high variance risk premium, which is typical of stressed markets, predicts *higher* returns up to about six months ahead, though with low explanatory power ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:22:y:2009:i:11:p:4463-4492); [Pyun poster](https://sjpyun.github.io/Poster.pdf)). My reading is that a high-volatility state is a mixture of crash days and rebound days. As a block it pays poorly for its risk, but the best days of the decade sit inside it, and no backward-looking volatility measure can tell which half of the mixture today belongs to.

### Bottoms are only dated after the fact

Even the signals built to mark bottoms fire after them. Paul Desmond's Dow Award study of Lowry's 90% days found that the 90% Upside Day (or a pair of 80% days) that confirms a major bottom came **within five trading days of the low in only about half the cases** across 69 years ([Scribd copy of the 2002 Dow Award paper](https://www.scribd.com/doc/240402654/2002-Dow-Award)). Breadth thrusts require a broad rally to be under way before they can trigger. Rob Hanna's independent test of follow-through days on the S&P 500 from 1971 to 2008 shows the other half of the problem. Under the original 1% rule every rally had a follow-through day, but **only 35 of 64 (54.7%) succeeded**. With the stricter 1.7% threshold the rate was 29 of 52 (55.7%), against IBD's claim of 70–80% ([Quantifiable Edges](https://quantifiableedges.com/follow-through-days-quantified/); [pt.1](https://quantifiableedges.com/ibd-follow-through-days-pt-1-are-they-predictive/)). Hanna also found follow-through days *less* reliable after large declines ([Quantifiable Edges](https://quantifiableedges.com/follow-through-days-quantified/)), which are the declines that matter to v5. Capitulation is not a reliable marker either: SentimenTrader notes that many bottoms came without it ([SentimenTrader](https://sentimentrader.com/blog/no-capitulation-no-breadth-thrust)), which fits v10's capitulation trigger never firing near the 2009, 2011 or 2022 lows.

### Bear-market rallies look like bottoms

The failures documented in practice are nearly all bear-market rallies. A looser version of the Zweig Breadth Thrust won in 130 of 132 signals on the Nasdaq 100 since 1985. Its two losses, **−35.08% after 2002-03-06 and −35.55% after 2008-02-01**, were both rallies inside secular bears ([SentimenTrader](https://sentimentrader.com/blog/about-that-near-miss-zweig-breadth-thrust)). Stop-loss research points the same way. Kaminski and Lo show that 0/1 stop rules lower expected return under a random walk and add value only when returns have momentum ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:234-254)). A later study found that stop-loss plus re-entry rules raised risk-adjusted returns in secular bear markets but not in bull markets ([EU-OpenSci](https://eu-opensci.org/index.php/ejbmr/article/view/51426)). VolStrat's own tests show the trap from three angles. A broad recovery re-entry rule fired 70 times in 20 years against 19 for the panic trigger, and many of its entries were 2008 bear-market rallies (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §15.5, data to 2026-11-03). v10 bought back too early in 2008 and deepened the 2008–09 drawdown to −18.4% from −15.2%. In v11 the fast/slow "rebound state" (252-day return negative, 42-day return positive), the academic favourite for turning points, was followed by **worse-than-average returns in both eras**: −1.30% vs +2.44% over 63 days on 1928–1989, and −2.3% vs +1.7% on 1991–2026 (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §22–§23, data to 2026-11-03). In other words, it kept buying rallies inside bear markets.

### Too few independent bottoms to tell skill from luck

Every re-entry rule is fitted and judged on a few dozen events at most. Vendors count the Zweig thrust as 16, 18, 19 or 20 signals since the 1940s ([RIA](https://realinvestmentadvice.com/resources/blog/a-rare-zweig-breadth-thrust-provides-optimism/); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/11/06/zweig-breadth-thrust-rare-market-buy-signal.html); [Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/market-just-did-something-only-073000463.html)). VolStrat's panic-rebound trigger fired 20 times in 20 years (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §15.2, data to 2026-11-03). v10 helped clearly in three sharp panics (2018-12, 2020, 2025-04), hurt in 2008, and was absent at three other lows, which is too few cases to separate a real edge from the order in which history happened to arrive. A rough calculation of my own makes the point. For two strategies whose returns are about 95% correlated, the standard error of their Sharpe difference over 20 years is roughly 0.07 (using the approximation √(2(1−ρ)/T), with T in years), which is larger than any gain any re-entry variant has shown. Seen this way, bootstrap win rates of 30–40% are what noise looks like.

## Outside evidence offers in-sample edges and vendor tallies, not a tested rule

No peer-reviewed study found in this research tests a re-entry rule out of sample across many independent sell-offs. What exists falls into the families below.

| Signal family | Strongest evidence | Quality | Fires relative to the low | Testable before 1990? | VolStrat result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| VIX term-structure inversion | Backwardation predicts positive S&P returns; contango carries no signal ([MDPI](https://mdpi.com/1911-8074/12/3/113)) | Peer-reviewed, in-sample, full text unverified | At or before | No (VIX3M from about 2007) | §15.2 passed on Growth; v10 failed on v5 |
| Variance risk premium | High premium predicts higher returns up to about 6 months ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:22:y:2009:i:11:p:4463-4492)) | Peer-reviewed, in-sample, low R² | At | No | Not tested as a trigger |
| Fast/slow momentum "Rebound" state | Dynamic speed 9.4% vs 7.5% a year at 10% vol, gross, 1971–2019 ([CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=34159)) | Peer-reviewed, out-of-sample status unclear | After | Yes | v11 P1: worst candidate in both eras |
| Volatility-managed scaling | Real-time versions of 103 strategies generally fail to beat unmanaged ones ([Cederburg et al.](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)) | Peer-reviewed, out of sample | Lags (same window) | Yes | v5 is this, with a brake |
| Follow-through day | About 55% success, 1971–2008 ([Quantifiable Edges](https://quantifiableedges.com/follow-through-days-quantified/)) | Independent practitioner | After | Yes | v11 P4: best 1928–89, below average 1991–2026 |
| Breadth thrusts (Zweig, Deemer) | S&P higher a year later in 23 of 24 Deemer signals ([Carson](https://www.carsongroup.com/insights/blog/staying-classy-market-breadth-buying-thrusts-and-brian-fantana)) | Vendor-compiled, no academic replication | After, often weeks | Yes, if data can be obtained | Untested (no free data) |
| High-yield spreads | Led equity *peaks* by a median of about 7 months, 1997–2026 ([eco3min](https://eco3min.fr/en/us-hy-credit-spread-leading-indicator-dataset/)) | Secondary, anecdotal at bottoms | Before (2009), with (2020) | Only via Baa spreads | Untested |

The implied-volatility evidence carries a twist that matters for v5. Where Fassas and Hourvouliades find a signal, it is in the *inverted* state itself. The return to contango, the moment v10 waits for, is the state they find uninformative ([MDPI](https://mdpi.com/1911-8074/12/3/113)). Practitioner data are less kind still. A study of weekly data from 2018 to 2026 found median forward returns almost identical in backwardation and contango, with forward-return dispersion about 60% higher during backwardation, and it recommends using the signal for sizing rather than direction ([FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/articles/vix-term-structure-inversions-since-2018-data-study)). Backwardation episodes since 2010 have lasted a median of two days ([thetrading.tools](https://www.thetrading.tools/vix-term-structure)), so "back to contango" fires often, including inside grinding bears. CXO Advisory's independent test of a VIX-spike buying rule over 1990–2007 found a real but small edge and a total return of **196% against 322% for buy-and-hold** ([CXO](https://cxoadvisory.com/sentiment-indicators/short-term-relative-vix-level-as-a-trading-signal)). Cheng finds that the VIX premium normalises only late in a stress episode ([OUP](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/32/1/180/5017289)), so waiting for it to normalise is a lagging signal.

The momentum turning-point work by Goulding, Harvey and Mazzoleni is the best-documented academic answer to slow re-entry, and its gains are modest. Dynamic speed earned 9.4% against 7.5% a year at 10% volatility over 30 years, gross of costs, with small Correction and Rebound subsamples ([CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=34159)). On US equities alone, a 2021 conference deck shows a dynamic Sharpe of about 0.52 against 0.51 for the best static speed. That reading is uncertain because the table was garbled when extracted ([AEA 2021 slides](https://benny.aeaweb.org/conference/2021/preliminary/powerpoint/ihbRDkeH)). Newfound's recovery simulations explain why speed choice is a trap. In a sharp V, very fast and very slow trend signals both cope, and intermediate speeds suffer most ([AdvisorAnalyst](https://advisoranalyst.com/?p=128173)). Breadth thrusts have the most striking records: Zweig signals before April 2025 had median S&P gains of 13.2% at six months and 24.8% at twelve, and the index was higher at both horizons every time ([Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/market-just-did-something-only-073000463.html)). Vendors compiled every one of those records, though, and signals have become more frequent in recent years ([Detrick on X](https://x.com/RyanDetrick/status/1825955327348715864)).

One inference ties this section together. **The best-supported outside signals (backwardation, a high variance risk premium, panic states) are contrarian buys into peak volatility.** A volatility budget cannot adopt them without ceasing to be a volatility budget. Buying at the moment of highest volatility is a different strategy, not a patch on v5, and the earlier VolStrat research reached a related conclusion: volatility signals size risk well and forecast returns poorly once tested in real time ([Cederburg et al.](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)).

## Five pre-registered fixes left the gap where it was

VolStrat has now tested each main family it can measure. The results are consistent.

| Test | Rule | Period | Sharpe (variant vs base) | P(better than base) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| §15.2 panic rebound (on Growth) | VIX/VIX3M > 1.10 within 20 days, then < 1.00: lift to Neutral for 20 days | 2007–2026 | 0.67 vs 0.60 | 92% | Passed, adopted into Growth |
| §15.5 broad recovery re-entry (on Growth) | VIX ≤ 0.8× 20-day high, contango, S&P above 20-day average | 2017–2026 test half | 0.84 vs 0.85 | 46% | Fail |
| v8 implied-vol sizing (W1 / W2 / W3) | Size by VIX, a blend, or VIX plus capitulation lift | 1991–2026 | 0.61 / 0.63 / 0.62 vs 0.66 | 1% / 2% / 6% | Fail |
| v10 capitulation floor | §15.2 trigger sets an 80% equity floor for 20 days | 2007–2026 | 0.67 vs 0.69 | 37% | Fail |
| v11 follow-through day | Trend brake lifted for 20 days after a ≥1.7% gain on day 4–10 after a 20-day low | 1993–2026 | 0.64 vs 0.65 | 30% | Fail |

Source: VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §15, §21–§23, data to 2026-11-03.

Three findings stand out. First, **the capitulation trigger that passed on Growth failed on v5**. In my reading, the difference lies in the base rule, not the trigger. Growth's 2007–2016 Sharpe was 0.46, while v5 held its 2008–09 drawdown to −15.2% and earned 0.68 over the same years, so v5 had less to repair. An early purchase in late 2008 cost v5 more than it saved, and v10's 2007–2016 Sharpe fell to 0.54. Its 2017–2026 Sharpe rose to 0.81 from 0.70, with three-month returns after the 2018-12, 2020 and 2025-04 lows of **10.3%, 9.3% and 14.5% against 6.2%, 6.6% and 8.6%**. Second, **signal rankings flip between eras**. The follow-through day was the best candidate on 1928–1989, with a mean forward 63-day return of 4.96% and 16.9% false alarms. On 1991–2026 it returned 1.1%, below the 1.7% average for all stress days. The momentum thrust had the fewest false alarms in 1991–2026 (7.8%) and the most on 1928–1989 (26.0%). Third, **opening one lock is not enough**. v8 showed that the volatility measure is not the binding constraint, because the trend brake halves exposure until the 200-day average is regained. v11 lifted the brake and moved median rebound capture only from 25.9% to 27.3%, because the volatility lock still held. The only design that opened both locks at once was v10's 80% floor, and that is the one that bought 2008 too early (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §22–§23, data to 2026-11-03).

One pattern is tempting and should be resisted. Twice now, holding the capitulation override until the curve re-inverts or a 20-day low breaks has looked better than a fixed 20-day hold. On Growth it won in 61–65% of resamples. On v5 it reached a Sharpe of 0.72 against 0.69 and won in 82% (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §15.5, §22–§23, data to 2026-11-03). Both versions were specified after their fixed-hold counterparts had been seen, and VIX3M history begins around 2007, so no unseen data remain on which to test the idea. It was rightly not adopted.

## What is worth doing: set the dial, glide back, test breadth only on clean data

The options below are ranked by how well the evidence supports them, not by how much they promise.

| Option | What it buys | Evidence status | Clean test possible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accept the lag; choose the target-vol and brake dial | More return in recoveries, deeper drawdowns, a modestly lower Sharpe ratio | Reported, not tested; settings chosen after seeing data | Not needed; it is a preference |
| Disclose the lag as a cost | Users know in advance that v5 holds roughly 10–25% equity for about two months after a crash | Descriptive (rule arithmetic and §22.1) | Not needed; already measured |
| Tranched glide back | Less timing regret; no expected return gain | Tranching reduces timing luck in other settings; untested here | Only partly: 1928–1989 was used in v11 |
| Breadth-thrust test | The one untested family with pre-1990 history | Vendor records only | Only if licensed NYSE advance/decline data can be obtained |
| Credit veto (Baa spread) | Blocks re-entry while spreads make new highs | Inference only | Yes in principle (data from 1919) |
| Judge on the live record | An honest verdict on implementation | Strongest available | Slowly: a few sell-offs per decade |

**The dial is the honest lever.** Raising v5's volatility target buys back recovery return directly, at a cost in drawdown. An 18% target with up to 1.5× exposure through SSO earned **11.4% a year against the S&P 500's 10.8%, with a −27.0% maximum drawdown against −55.2%**, over 1991–2026. Its Sharpe of 0.60 was below the 12% preset's 0.65. These settings were chosen after seeing the data, so they are a risk choice, not a test result (VolStrat tests, METHODOLOGY §21, data to 2026-11-03). Softening the brake, for example cutting exposure by a third rather than half below the 200-day average, is the other dial. It would speed re-entry, but it weakens the component that added 0.14 Sharpe on 1927–1989, a period that includes the slow bear markets of 1929–1932 and 1973–1974. If it is offered, it should be shown with that cost attached, not presented as an improvement. Equally useful, and cheap, is to show the lag on the site. On the rule's own arithmetic, v5 will hold roughly 10–25% equity through the first two months of a V-shaped recovery. In my judgment, a user who knows that in advance is less likely to abandon the strategy at the worst moment than one who finds out live.

**A tranched glide path is the most defensible design change, and it is a smoothing device, not an edge.** One version would raise an equity floor by a fixed step each week after realised volatility peaks, cap it below full exposure, and cancel it on a new 20-day low. Spreading entries over time does not require identifying the bottom, which is the one thing nothing can do, and it limits the damage when a bear-market rally fails. The case for tranching comes from rebalance-timing luck: Newfound shows that choice of rebalance date alone produces 100–400 basis points of annual dispersion in rule-based portfolios, and recommends correcting "a little but frequently" ([Newfound](https://blog.thinknewfound.com/tag/rebalance-timing-luck/)). VolStrat's own blending tests point the same way: three-sleeve blends beat the average of their own sleeves by only 0.01–0.09% a year, reducing luck and drawdown without adding return (VolStrat backtests, data to 2026-11-03). Expectations should be set accordingly. v5 already is a continuous tranche keyed to volatility, so a time-based glide only changes its speed. Any test would also be compromised, because the 1928–1989 stress-day statistics were examined during v11's discovery stage. The same caveat applies to a realised-volatility analogue of the VIX capitulation trigger (a short-window volatility spike above long-window volatility, then reversing). That is the only way to test the capitulation idea before 2007, but it would run on data the project has already seen.

**Breadth is the one untested family, and its obstacle is data, not ideas.** Breadth thrusts fire closest to bottoms in vendor studies, and they are the only bottom signal with history before 1990. But no free daily NYSE advance/decline history going back decades was found. QuantShare offers data from March 1965 behind a login ([QuantShare](https://www.quantshare.com/item-151-advancing-declining-unchanged-volume-for-nyse-amex-and-nasdaq)). Unicorn data via Wealth-Lab is being moved or decommissioned ([Wealth-Lab](https://wealth-lab.com/Forum/Posts/30921)). CRSP is institutional and paid ([HBS](https://www.library.hbs.edu/Find/Databases/Center-for-Research-in-Security-Prices-CRSP)). Modern NYSE counts include ETFs and preferreds, which may explain why signals have clustered since 2023 ([Detrick on X](https://x.com/RyanDetrick/status/1825955327348715864)). A constituent-panel substitute would carry survivorship bias. Even with good data the prior should be low. v11's momentum thrust, the closest price analogue of a breadth thrust, flipped from worst to best between eras, and the thrusts' known failures (2002, 2008) are exactly the bear-market rallies v5 must avoid. If the project licenses data, a single pre-registered test on 1965–1989 is defensible, because the breadth signal itself would be unseen even though the price path is not. It should state an event count in advance and expect a change of a few points in rebound capture. Credit belongs in the same category. Spreads appear more useful as a veto than as a trigger, since they reportedly recovered about 2.5 months before equities in 2009 ([Hedge Fund Alpha](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/the-high-yield-credit-cycle-what-you-need-to-know/)). FRED's high-yield series now keeps only three years of history ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/BAMLEMHGHGLCRPIUSTRIV)), so a long test would have to use Baa spreads, an untested substitute.

**The live record is the final judge, and it will be slow.** The project has run more than 30 designs on 1993–2026 and has now used 1927–1989 for both v5's test and v11's discovery. No clean US daily history remains for further re-entry searches, so every new variant on that data adds to the multiple-testing burden. The v5 live record (v3) will see perhaps a few sell-offs per decade. That is enough to check that the rule behaves as backtested, and not enough to validate a re-entry rule. Changing v5 now would restart the record for a gain that no test has shown.

## Conclusion

The re-entry gap is not a defect waiting for the right indicator. It is the visible half of the trade that makes v5 work. The days v5 avoids and the days it misses are largely the same days, and no signal tested, inside VolStrat or out, sorts them consistently once it is moved to a different era. The literature's most credible re-entry signals point in a direction v5 cannot follow without contradicting itself: buying hardest when volatility is highest. The real choice is therefore between strategies, not between triggers. The cost of the volatility budget's protection can only be priced and disclosed, not engineered away.

That moves the question from research to product. The useful next steps are to let users choose how much drawdown protection to pay for (target volatility, brake strength), to show them the expected re-entry lag before they need it, and to stop searching data that has already been seen. The one study worth funding is a single, pre-registered breadth test on licensed pre-1990 data, with modest expectations. Everything else should wait for the live record.

## Addendum (2026-11-05): breadth thrusts tested on a century of data

The one untested family, breadth thrusts, was tested using the share of Ken French's 49 US
industry portfolios rising each day, 1926–2026, pre-registered in `docs/PREREG-v12.md`
(METHODOLOGY §24). After 1990 the Zweig- and Deemer-style thrusts were strong: on stress days
they were followed by mean 63-day returns of 4.9% and 4.5% (all stress days: 2.3%) with
false-alarm rates of 6.0% and 6.7% (13.0%). Before 1990 they were no better than doing nothing
(1.9% and 0.9% against 2.4%), and neither fired within three months of the 1974, 1987, 2002,
2009 or 2022 lows. Added to v5 they would have raised 1990–2026 CAGR by 0.7–0.8 points, at the
cost of deeper drawdowns and a clearly lower Sharpe ratio on 1928–1989. All five re-entry
families tested now show the same era split, so the conclusion above stands: choose the
trade-off rather than chase bottoms.
